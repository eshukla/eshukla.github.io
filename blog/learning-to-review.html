Learning to Review

Estuti Shukla April 2026

I recently got my first opportunity to review a paper for a journal that I had previously published in. It was a mix of excitement and anxiety - I felt honored to be considered as a reviewer but I was worried that since the topic of the paper wasn't closely related to my research, I won't be able to provide a meaningful review. After giving it a lot of thought, I decided to ask the journal to let me co-review this paper with my advisor and they were happy to oblige. I found out that this co-review opportunity is especially designed to help early-career researchers learn how to review papers under the supervision of someone who has ideally done this multiple times before. In this way, both the reviewers are acknowldeged for their work by the journal without putting too much pressure on early career researchers. So if you are someone who is also doing this for the first time, try contacting the editor to ask about their co-review policy.

I decided to write about this mostly to gather my thoughts, resources that I found online and what I learnt in this process (just in case someone finds this useful).

Here are some of the most useful resources I found online-

  • How to referee a research paper by Dave Gifford
  • Wiley's guideline to reviewing a manuscript
  • Good old reddit
This is definitely not an exhaustive list but just some of the resources I used to familiarize myself with the process.

What I learnt

Peer-review process is meant to highlight important contributions of the manuscript to the editor, especially to help them contextualise important contributions, novelty and limitations of the work. It helps the editor decide whether the manuscript meets the journal standards. It also helps the authors to improve their manuscript and get feedback. Personally, I took this as an opportunity to learn something different from my usual research projects.

One important thing to keep in mind is that you aren't writing the review for the authors, but for the editor. In the beginning, write a paragraph or two summarising the work to the editor. This can be followed by giving some context as to what this work contributes to our current understanding of the field and whether this would be a suitable contribution to the readers of this particular journal. You can keep also keep confidential comments for the editor. Finally, you can ask authors (politely and professionally) to clarify or change things. If I am suggesting changes, I would also give tips on how I would like them to do things differently instead of vague suggestions.

What I did

When I read the paper for the first time, I made a note of all the questions that were coming to my mind. This should give you an initial impression of the paper and an idea of whether you would advise the editor to accept,reject or revise the manuscript. After this I wrote my initial summary in a paragraph about what the authors are doing in this work and what new findings does the paper introduce. Since this was a relatively new topic for me, I went back and read few papers that were cited by the authors in their literature review section to understand the bigger picture.
